EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
-----------------
This report presents findings from a structured SQL-based analysis of 200
incidents logged by a 250-person IT services company. The analysis measures
current ITSM performance against BRD-defined targets, identifies root causes
of SLA breaches, and provides prioritised recommendations for improvement.

Current state: Overall MTTR at 17.6h (weighted across all priorities) and
SLA compliance at 84.0%. The headline MTTR figure is influenced heavily by
the P3/P4 incident mix. The critical finding is that P1 and P2 incidents —
the highest impact tickets — are failing SLA at alarming rates of 54.2% and
36.4% respectively, while P3 and P4 incidents achieve 100% compliance.

Finding 1 — P2 is the single biggest SLA failure at 36.4% compliance.
21 out of 33 P2 incidents breached SLA. P2 incidents carry an 8-hour
target — tight enough to miss under workload pressure but not urgent
enough to trigger emergency escalation. They fall into a gap in the
response model.

Finding 2 — P1 compliance at 54.2% is unacceptable for critical incidents.
11 out of 24 P1 incidents breached their SLA. These are the highest-impact
tickets in the system. An organisation cannot claim effective incident
management when it fails more than half its critical incidents.

Finding 3 — P3 and P4 achieve perfect SLA compliance.
Both tiers hit 100% compliance, largely because their SLA windows (24h
and 48h) are generous relative to actual resolution times of 16.3h and
29.8h. This is positive but masks the P1/P2 crisis.

SECTION 5: ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------

Root Cause 1 — No P1/P2 escalation fast-track (highest impact)
The data shows P3/P4 achieving 100% SLA while P1/P2 fail badly. This
indicates the organisation lacks a dedicated fast-track escalation path
for critical incidents. P1/P2 tickets enter the same queue as lower
priority work and compete for engineer attention.
Evidence: P1 at 54.2%, P2 at 36.4% vs P3/P4 at 100%.

Root Cause 2 — No KEDB for recurring Database and Server incidents
Database (10 breaches) and Server (9 breaches) account for 59% of all
SLA failures. These categories have recurring incident patterns —
connection failures, query degradation, NTP sync failures — that are
likely being re-diagnosed each time instead of resolved via documented
known-error fixes.
Evidence: Database 75.0% SLA, Server 71.9% SLA, highest breach counts.

Root Cause 3 — Uneven workload distribution
Two engineers carry 40 incidents each while one carries only 25. Uneven
distribution leads to queue backlog for overloaded engineers, directly
increasing MTTR for their assigned tickets.
Evidence: P. Nair at 25 assigned vs R. Sharma and S. Patil at 40 each.

Root Cause 4 — No real-time SLA visibility
Without a live dashboard, P1/P2 breaches are discovered after the fact.
The resolution window closes before anyone acts. Real-time alerting at
the 50% and 75% SLA consumption mark would allow proactive intervention.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 8: CONCLUSION
------------------------------------------------

The data exposes a split-personality ITSM operation: excellent at managing
low-priority, low-urgency tickets (P3/P4 at 100% SLA) and poor at managing
the tickets that matter most to the business (P1 at 54.2%, P2 at 36.4%).

This is not a capacity problem — it is a process and visibility problem.
The engineers are present and resolving tickets. The failure is in how
critical incidents are triaged, escalated, and tracked in real time.

The three interventions that would close the gap fastest:

1. Dedicated P1/P2 escalation queue — immediate impact on the 58.6pt P2 gap
2. Real-time dashboard with SLA countdown alerts — prevents breaches
   from going unnoticed until after the window closes
3. KEDB for Server and Database — eliminates re-diagnosis of known issues
   which account for 59% of all SLA breaches

With these three changes in place, achieving 95% overall SLA compliance
within one quarter is realistic based on the current data trajectory.
